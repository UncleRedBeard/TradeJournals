# Guest Bath and Dresser Vanity

A compact bathroom is rarely a simple decorating project. Plumbing, fixtures,
lighting, wall surfaces, trim, moisture, and daily use all occupy the same
small footprint. In an old house, the challenge is to make those systems work
without flattening the room into something generic.

This bath retained the elements that already tied it to the house: beadboard,
small-format floor tile, tall windows, the clawfoot tub, and the surrounding
trim. The central fitment problem was the vanity. An antique dresser offered
the right visual weight and useful storage, but it was furniture rather than a
ready-made bathroom cabinet. Adapting it meant planning around the vessel sink,
drain, supply lines, top, drawers, and the need for future access.

The photographs show the dresser being prepared and finished, then the sink
being clamped and fitted into place. That intermediate work matters as much as
the room-ready view. It shows that the vanity was not dropped into the room as
an untouched object. It was altered deliberately so the plumbing and sink
could function while the dresser still read as a piece of furniture.

The final room brings the pieces back together. Mirror and wall lights sit
between the windows above the dresser. The vessel sink establishes the modern
working surface, while the beadboard, tile, tub, and retained trim keep the
room connected to the rest of the residence.

The public evidence supports the visible sequence of surface repair, vanity
preparation, sink fitting, and return to use. It does not reveal every hidden
plumbing, wiring, or waterproofing decision, so the story does not claim more
than the archive shows. What it does show is a practical kind of preservation:
making a distinctive old object carry a new function, and making that function
belong in the room.
