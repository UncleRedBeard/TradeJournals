# Returning to Clay

Returning to wheel-thrown pottery after roughly thirty years has made the old
work newly useful. Thirty-five archive photographs record eight surviving
vessels made circa 1994–1995 while studying ceramics at Brookhaven Community
College. They were thrown on a Lockerbie Model K and fired in a wood kiln that
I helped build.

The tall jar is the strongest overall form from that period. Its scale, quiet
volume, and wood-fired surface support one another without asking the firing to
rescue the silhouette. A pale bowl is the strongest functional piece: its
openness, rim, and surface carry the same direct gesture. Other surviving forms
are more unresolved, especially where heavy rims, added-looking feet, or
competing parts interrupt the vessel as a whole.

That critique gives the current practice direction. The present kickwheel is
the same make and model as the earlier Lockerbie, though it is not the same
physical wheel. The goal is to recover muscle memory while paying closer
attention to rims, feet, transitions, proportion, and the way an alteration
strengthens or weakens the original volume.

The current photographed work is still greenware. An incised Balcones Dark jar
shows the emerging surface vocabulary: repeated marks follow the lower volume
rather than sitting on it as decoration. These pieces have not yet passed
through bisque and glaze firing, so their finished color and surface remain an
open question.

Clay sits outside the website's primary restoration portfolio, but the working
method is closely related. Both ask what a material already wants to do, what
should be retained, and where intervention earns its place. The older pots
provide evidence rather than nostalgia. They preserve the instincts worth
recovering and the habits that still need correction as the apprenticeship
begins again.
